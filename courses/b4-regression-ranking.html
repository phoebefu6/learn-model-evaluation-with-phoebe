Part 1 · covers sklearn regression metrics + the which-scoring-function doctrine

Six regression metrics, one question 15 min live

Every regression metric answers the same question - which errors hurt? - with a different opinion. MAE says all dollars hurt equally. RMSE says big misses hurt disproportionately. MAPE says percentage misses hurt, wherever they happen. Learn the opinion, not just the formula, and the choice becomes obvious from the business.

MetricThe question it answersProsThe trap
mean_absolute_error (MAE)How big is the typical miss, in $?Reads in real dollars; robust to a few wild missesTreats a $10 and a $10,000 miss linearly - no extra alarm for disasters

LiveThe which-scoring-function doctrine4 min▶

sklearn's evaluation guide compresses this whole zoo into one decision rule - what are you actually trying to predict?

  • Predicting a mean - score with squared error (RMSE family).
  • Predicting a median - score with absolute error (MAE family).
  • Predicting a quantile (p90 demand for safety stock) - score with pinball loss.
  • Predicting a probability - score with proper scoring rules (Brier, log loss - b3's territory).

The doctrine cuts both ways: a model trained toward the mean but reported on MAE is being graded against a target it never aimed at. Say what the model predicts, then the metric picks itself.

Baseline discipline (intro-ml canon) ALWAYS report regression metrics against the predict-the-mean baseline. "MAE $19" means nothing alone; "MAE $19 vs $31 for predict-the-mean" is a model earning its keep. Same paradox-proofing as b1's always-say-no model - the dumb baseline is the lie detector.
LiveR² ~0.45 and proud of it4 min▶

The intro-ml course's order-value model reached R² ~0.45 with multiple features - and that is honest work on noisy humans. Individual purchase behavior is mostly unexplainable variance; nobody's features capture mood, payday, or a friend's recommendation. Two habits follow:

  • Calibrate expectations by domain. R² 0.45 on human behavior can be excellent; R² 0.98 on a physical sensor can be mediocre. There is no universal "good R²."
  • Never read R² alone. High R² with structured residuals is common - the model can explain the variance and still be systematically wrong for a whole segment (every order above $200, say). Residual plots are part of the metric, not an optional extra.
Real world

The demand model with the beautiful R². A retailer's demand forecast reported R² 0.91 and shipped. The residuals, never plotted, were fine on ordinary weeks and wildly low every promotion week - exactly the weeks the forecast existed for. The aggregate metric averaged over the moments that mattered. RMSE sliced by promotion weeks would have caught it in review.

Self-studyThe regression six in sklearn, 60 seconds2 min read▶

The intro-ml course covers the mechanics hands-on; here is the whole family as a refresher, with the baseline wired in:

import numpy as np
from sklearn.metrics import (
    mean_absolute_error, root_mean_squared_error,
    mean_absolute_percentage_error, r2_score,
    median_absolute_error, mean_pinball_loss,
)

mae  = mean_absolute_error(y_test, pred)
rmse = root_mean_squared_error(y_test, pred)
mape = mean_absolute_percentage_error(y_test, pred)  # guard zeros first
r2   = r2_score(y_test, pred)
med  = median_absolute_error(y_test, pred)
p90  = mean_pinball_loss(y_test, pred_q90, alpha=0.9)

# the lie detector: the predict-the-mean baseline
baseline = np.full_like(y_test, y_test.mean(), dtype=float)
mae_base = mean_absolute_error(y_test, baseline)

One habit worth stealing: print every metric as a pair - model vs baseline - in the same log line. Reviews go faster when the lie detector is already in the output.

Part 2 · covers sklearn ndcg_score + average_precision_score

Ranking metrics - when position matters 14 min live

Search results, recommendations, top-k outreach lists: the model's output is an ORDER, and errors near the top hurt more than errors near the bottom. Classification metrics ignore position entirely; ranking metrics make it the whole point.

LiveThe ranking family in four names6 min▶
  • precision@k - of the top k items, how many are relevant? The metric of every budget cutoff: "we can act on 500, how many are real?"
  • recall@k - of all relevant items, how many made the top k? The metric of coverage: "how many real buyers does the top 500 even contain?"
  • NDCG - handles GRADED relevance (a perfect match beats an okay match), discounts each position by log2 of its rank so the top of the list dominates, and normalizes to 0-1 against the ideal ordering. In sklearn: ndcg_score with k= for a cutoff. Canonical citation: Jarvelin & Kekalainen 2002.
  • average_precision_score - sklearn's AP summarizes the whole precision-recall tradeoff of the ranking. It is deliberately step-wise, not trapezoidal: linear interpolation of PR curves is overly optimistic (the b3 warning, still true here).

Honest note: true IR-style MAP@k - averaged over queries the way search papers report it - is not in sklearn. If a vendor quotes MAP@10, ask exactly how they computed it.

LiveLumen's top-500 list: precision@500 IS the business metric8 min▶

Here is how ranking metrics enter Lumen's story: rank all sessions by the conversion model's score, and marketing's outreach budget covers the top 500. Nobody below position 500 gets touched, so the quality of the whole ranking is irrelevant - precision@500 is not a proxy for the business metric, it IS the business metric: real converters per 500 outreach dollars spent. This is the rare, happy case where the metric and the money are the same number.

import numpy as np
from sklearn.metrics import ndcg_score

# graded relevance (0-3) vs model scores, top-heavy by design
ndcg = ndcg_score([relevance], [scores], k=500)

# precision@k by hand: slice the top k, count the hits
k = 500
top_k = np.argsort(scores)[::-1][:k]
precision_at_k = y_true[top_k].sum() / k
The offline-online gap, previewed Offline ranking metrics correlate WEAKLY with online clicks - a list that scores better on NDCG does not reliably earn more engagement from real users. The A/B test remains the truth; the offline metric is the filter that decides what is worth testing. b9 names this the two-hop proxy chain and builds the governance around it.
Self-studyReading NDCG without fear3 min read▶

NDCG intimidates people because the formula has three moving parts. Read them one at a time and it is friendly:

  • Graded relevance. Items are not just relevant/irrelevant - a 3 (perfect) beats a 1 (marginal). Binary labels still work; grades are the upgrade.
  • The log2 rank discount. Position 1 counts full weight, position 2 divided by log2(3), and so on - relevance buried at rank 40 is worth almost nothing. The discount encodes the belief that users read from the top.
  • The N is normalization. The score is divided by the best achievable ordering of the same items, so 1.0 always means "the ideal ranking" and scores compare across queries.

Two reading rules: an NDCG without a stated k is a whole-list score (rarely what the business does), and an NDCG compared across DIFFERENT datasets means little - the normalizer changes with the items. Compare rankers on the same data, at your real k.

Part 3 · the 30-second chooser

Choosing in 30 seconds self-study

The whole session as a flowchart in prose. Say the target out loud, walk the branches, done.

Self-studyThe flowchart, in prose3 min read▶
  • Money target? Start with MAE and RMSE together, then pick by outlier pain: if one huge miss breaks something (inventory, capacity), lead with RMSE; if you want the honest typical miss, lead with MAE (or median absolute error when the tail is pure noise).
  • Comparing across scales? (stores, products, currencies) - MAPE, with a zero-guard: exclude or floor near-zero actuals first, and remember it penalizes over-forecast less.
  • Ranked list with a budget cutoff? - precision@k at YOUR k. The cutoff is a business fact, not a modeling choice.
  • Graded relevance, order matters everywhere? - NDCG (with k if only the top matters).

And in every branch: quote the number NEXT TO its dumb baseline (predict-the-mean, or a random ranking). A metric without a baseline is a mood.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Lumen forecasts order values to size warehouse capacity, where one huge miss breaks the packing line. RMSE or MAE?

RMSE's squaring is not a quirk, it is the opinion: disasters count extra. When one big miss hurts more than many small ones (inventory, capacity), that opinion matches the business. When it does not, MAE or the median tells the truer story.

2 · MAPE has two famous traps. Which pair is it?

Divide by an actual near zero and one row can dominate the whole average; and the same $ miss scores differently over- vs under-forecast. MAPE earns its keep on cross-scale comparison - guard the zeros and know the asymmetry.

3 · Marketing's budget covers the top 500 sessions on the list. When does precision@500 beat AUC as the metric to watch?

AUC scores the entire ordering, including the 90% of the list below the budget line. When action stops at k, precision@k is real converters per outreach dollar - the metric and the money are the same number. More information is not better when it is information about sessions you will never contact.
