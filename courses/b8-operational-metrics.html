Operational metrics

Quality metrics tell you whether the answer is good. Tonight's metrics tell you whether anyone will wait for it and whether you can afford to serve it. You will learn the NVIDIA serving vocabulary - TTFT, ITL, the identity that ties them together - plus per-query cost arithmetic and the discipline of budgeting p95, not averages. Then the payoff: latency and price go INSIDE the eval sheet as first-class success criteria, completing the satisficing architecture b5 started. A system with rising judge scores and 8-second answers is not a good system - it is a churn machine with a nice dashboard.

Part 1 · covers NVIDIA NIM benchmarking metrics (TTFT / ITL / TPS)

The serving math 8 min live

The NVIDIA benchmarking definitions, exactly - these are the industry's shared vocabulary, so learn them as written. TTFT (time to first token) = submit -> first token; it contains the queue wait, the prefill of your prompt, and the network hop, and it owns the "is it thinking?" UX moment. ITL (inter-token latency, also called TPOT) = (e2e - TTFT) / (output_tokens - 1) - the decode smoothness, how fluid the streaming feels. And the identity that ties them: e2e = TTFT + (tokens - 1) x ITL. Sanity-check any vendor claim with it - the three numbers must reconcile, and when they do not, that is your first question.

★ What you walk out with today The TTFT / ITL / TPS vocabulary read straight from the NVIDIA definitions, the identity as a vendor lie-detector, the batching tradeoff (system throughput vs each user's experience), per-query cost arithmetic with its four levers, and latency + price written into the eval sheet as owner-signed constraints.
LiveSystem TPS vs per-user TPS: the batching tradeoff4 min▶

Two throughput numbers that vendors love to blur: system TPS = total output tokens / wall time, across ALL concurrent requests - the infra bill's metric. Per-user TPS - what one person actually experiences in their stream. Under batching they diverge, by design: batching more requests together raises system throughput while degrading each user's ITL.

  • Big batches: cheaper per token, choppier for every individual user.
  • Small batches: smoother streams, a larger serving bill.
  • There is no correct setting - you choose, per product. A batch-overnight summarizer and a live chat assistant should not share a batching config, or a latency budget.

When a vendor quotes one glorious TPS number, ask which one it is, and at what concurrency. The identity from the figure catches the rest.

LivePercentile discipline: budget p95, never averages3 min▶

Latency distributions have long right tails: most requests are fine, and a few are terrible. An average blends the terrible into the fine and reports "fine." Users do not experience your average - the one 9-second answer is what they remember and what they quote in the churn survey.

So the budget is written in percentiles: p95 TTFT <= 800ms means 95% of requests see a first token inside 800ms, and you are watching the slowest 5% explicitly. For anything user-facing, budget p95 or p99; use the average only for capacity planning. One slow tail kills perceived quality - and the average will never tell you it is happening.

Self-studyThe identity as three lines of Python2 min read▶

Worth keeping in a scratch notebook for vendor meetings:

# e2e = TTFT + (tokens - 1) x ITL - reconcile any latency claim
ttft, itl, out_tokens = 0.42, 0.03, 120   # seconds, seconds, tokens
e2e = ttft + (out_tokens - 1) * itl
print(f"implied e2e: {e2e:.2f}s")          # 3.99s - does the claimed e2e match?

If a datasheet claims sub-second answers with those components, the numbers do not reconcile - someone is quoting TTFT as e2e, or averaging away the tail. Either way, you now have the question.

Part 2 · the money column

Cost per query, and the ROI arithmetic 8 min live

The cost of one query is not mysterious: tokens in x input price + tokens out x output price + retries. What teams skip is multiplying it by volume and putting it next to the quality number - which is the only place it means anything. The levers, in rough order of cheapness: model tier per step (not every step needs the big model), prompt caching (stop paying full price for the same system prompt), output-length discipline (you pay per output token; rambling is a line item), and max-turn caps (an agent that loops is a bill that loops).

The lesson is not the verdict - it is that this is a product decision, and it belongs on the sheet, made by someone who can see both columns. The general rule of thumb: a 2% quality win at 5x cost is usually a loss.

Compute your own version: your assistant's average tokens in/out, your models' prices, your monthly volume. One spreadsheet row per candidate model.

Add the retry rate - a 10% retry rate is a 10% surcharge on everything, and it hides in logs, not in price sheets.

Now apply one lever (caching, shorter outputs, or a cheaper model on the easy step) and recompute. The cheapest quality point is usually the one you did not have to buy back.

Part 3 · covers Anthropic define-success, Ng satisficing vs optimizing

Latency and price go inside the eval sheet 6 min live

Anthropic's own define-success guidance lists latency and price among its success-criteria categories - they are not infra footnotes, they are eval criteria with the same standing as quality. The instrument for holding them together is Ng's satisficing-vs-optimizing pattern from b5, now completed for an LLM system: one metric to maximize, everything else a hard constraint.

LiveThe satisficing architecture, completed5 min▶

The Lumen assistant's eval sheet, in one sentence:

maximize   task success on the golden set
subject to faithfulness >= 0.9
           p95 TTFT     <= 800ms
           cost         <= $0.02/query

Three rules that make it real rather than decorative:

  • Every constraint is owner-signed. Support owns the faithfulness floor, product owns the latency budget, finance owns the cost cap. A constraint nobody signed is a suggestion.
  • Constraints are pass/fail, not tradeable. A model that scores 0.95 on quality but blows the latency budget does not ship - that is the entire point of writing it down.
  • The optimizing metric stays singular. One number to climb keeps iteration fast (Ng's single-number doctrine); the constraints keep the climb from wandering somewhere expensive.
Real world

The assistant that got better and lost its users. A team shipped an assistant and watched judge scores rise release after release - richer answers, better citations, longer reasoning. Answers also crept from 2 seconds to 8. Users churned to the old search box. The postmortem's one line: latency was never a success criterion, so nobody was failing - every dashboard was green while the product died. One row on the eval sheet would have caught it in week one.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · Users complain the assistant "sits there thinking forever" before anything appears, but once text starts it flows fine. Which metric owns that complaint?

The dead air before the first character is TTFT's territory - queue, prefill, network. ITL owns the opposite complaint: text that starts fast but stutters as it streams. Matching the user's complaint to the right metric is what makes the fix findable.

2 · A vendor claims: e2e 1.5s, TTFT 400ms, 100 output tokens, ITL 30ms. What does the identity say?

e2e = TTFT + (tokens - 1) x ITL = 0.4 + 99 x 0.03 = 3.37s - more than double the claimed 1.5s. Maybe the token count is smaller, maybe the ITL is a best case, maybe e2e is a p50 while the rest are means. The identity does not tell you which - it tells you to ask.

3 · Why does the latency budget say "p95 TTFT <= 800ms" instead of "average TTFT <= 800ms"?

Most requests are fine and a few are terrible; the average blends them and reports "fine." A p95 budget watches the slowest 5% explicitly - and one slow tail kills perceived quality faster than a mediocre median ever will. Averages are for capacity planning, percentiles are for users.
